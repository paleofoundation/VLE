# A category standard needs a retest schedule

A standard should tell a buyer what is being evaluated and how a decision is made. It should also tell the licensee what happens next. A favorable result without a defined scope can become a claim about materials it never covered. A favorable result without an ongoing obligation can remain in circulation long after the circumstances supporting it have changed.

That is why our claim is a standard per product category with scheduled retesting. The category defines the subject of the standard. The schedule defines when evidence must be revisited under that standard. Both need to be stated plainly enough that someone outside the organization can understand the obligation.

Neither is adequately conveyed by a badge that says tested. The badge leaves the buyer to supply the meaning. A standard should do that work itself.

## Category is part of the rule

FDA's January 2025 guidance for lead in certain processed foods intended for babies and young children distinguishes food categories, including dry infant cereals and single-ingredient root vegetables. Its scope excludes infant formula, beverages and specified snack foods. Those distinctions are part of the guidance, not optional details. [FDA guidance, Introduction][FDA-LEAD]

This is why copying a limit without its category is a poor starting point for a sourcing standard. The number may be accurately transcribed while its application is wrong. The buyer still needs to know which material, intended use and jurisdiction the rule addresses. A familiar ingredient name is not enough to resolve those questions.

The same applies to the legal role of a source. A regulator's guidance, a statutory requirement and a private certification standard should not be presented as interchangeable. A program needs to explain what it has adopted, why it applies and what remains outside its scope. An impressive list of agencies is not an explanation.

## Publish the decision rule before using it

A category standard should be available before the result is judged. The reviewer needs a defined analyte scope, an applicable source for any limit, the reporting basis and the evidence requirements. The licensee needs to know what counts as sufficient evidence and what happens when a requirement is unmet.

These are recommendations for a defensible program, not claims that a particular regulator has prescribed this entire workflow. They establish a basic safeguard: the standard cannot be adjusted quietly to accommodate a result that has already arrived.

If a limit has not been established for the intended category, that gap must remain explicit. A number from another material should not be moved into the empty cell because the table looks unfinished. An unresolved standard is a reason to continue the standards work. It is not permission to manufacture certainty.

## Retesting needs an owner and a trigger

Scheduled retesting is an obligation that can be checked. A workable schedule identifies its scope, the rule that creates the obligation, the responsible licensee and the due date or due-date calculation. It also explains the consequence of missing the requirement. Without those details, a date can become decorative.

This article does not prescribe a testing interval. The interval belongs in the approved category standard and its supporting rationale. Different programs must not inherit a schedule simply because a form already contains a convenient default. The correct calendar cannot be inferred from the existence of a previous report.

The schedule also needs a relationship to change. If the material, production circumstances or evidence changes, the program needs a defined review path. The question is whether the existing decision still applies. Waiting for a routine date should not be a way to avoid examining a known change that could affect the decision.

## A method has limits too

The standard must be usable with the evidence it requests. EFSA's explanation of detection limits makes a basic distinction: detecting a substance and quantifying it are not the same capability. A method's sensitivity matters when interpreting what a report does and does not show. [EFSA detection-limit explanation][EFSA-LOD]

For a program reviewer, the practical question is whether the submitted evidence can support the specified decision. If it cannot, the answer should remain unresolved. Replacing an incomplete finding with a favorable status does not improve the measurement. It only hides the reason that more work is needed.

The laboratory report should therefore retain its original terms and qualifications. The program's interpretation should be recorded separately. A buyer should be able to distinguish what the laboratory reported from what the program concluded.

## Preserve the version that governed the decision

A standard will need revision when its basis or requirements change. That does not justify rewriting the history of an earlier decision. Preserve the version used, the evidence reviewed and the scope approved. State how a new version affects existing approvals and future retesting.

VLE's existing model keeps a qualification decision tied to a frozen profile version. Its editorial promise must be equally precise. A supplier file is not a qualification decision, and a retest date is not proof of compliance. The date records an obligation or supplier statement whose meaning still has to be established by the applicable standard.

A buyer should be able to understand the present status without losing the history. A licensee should be able to understand the next obligation without guessing at an unwritten policy. That is what a standard per product category with scheduled retesting is supposed to accomplish: a defined scope, an inspectable rule, and a continuing responsibility that remains visible after the first favorable result.
