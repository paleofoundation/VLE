# Lot data and published limits answer different questions

A published limit tells a buyer something about the rule. A laboratory result tells the buyer something about a sample. A lot record tells the buyer which material is being discussed. Sourcing requires a defensible connection between those records. None can quietly stand in for the others.

That connection is easy to lose on a page built for quick comparison. A product name, a testing claim and a document icon can look complete. Yet the buyer may still be unable to say whether the document applies to the offered material or whether the cited limit applies to its intended use. The page has supplied the appearance of an answer while leaving the decision unresolved.

## Identify the material before interpreting the claim

The first useful question is ordinary: what, exactly, is being offered? The ingredient name is only the beginning. The buyer needs a record that identifies the physical material and can be connected to the relevant sample and evidence. Otherwise, even a well-prepared report may describe something different from the proposed purchase.

FDA explains that a traceability lot code identifies a lot within the records of its source. It also describes linking the code to the key records associated with tracking events. This is traceability guidance within the rule's defined scope, not a universal certification system. [FDA traceability lot-code explanation][FDA-TRACE]

The sourcing implication is practical: preserve the connection. If the supplier, laboratory and buyer use different identifiers, the reviewer needs an explicit reconciliation. A similar name is not enough. If the relationship cannot be established, do not label it established merely to finish the approval form.

## Preserve what the laboratory actually reported

A report should arrive with the analyte, method, sample description, date, units and relevant qualifications intact. The reviewer needs to see the result in the context supplied by the laboratory. A transcription into a purchasing spreadsheet should not erase the original meaning.

For example, a non-detect is not a measured zero. EFSA describes the detection limit as the concentration a method can reliably detect, while distinguishing that capability from quantification. That is why a result needs its method context. [EFSA detection-limit explanation][EFSA-LOD]

The next question is whether the report can support the decision required by the applicable standard. If the method or reporting information is missing, ask for it. If the sample identity is unclear, resolve it. An attractive summary should not make those gaps harder to see than they were in the original report.

## Keep the published limit attached to its scope

A limit should travel with its source, category, jurisdiction, units and version. Remove those details and it becomes a number that can be placed beside almost anything. The appearance of a comparison is not evidence that the comparison is valid.

FDA's lead guidance for certain processed foods intended for babies and young children explicitly describes itself as nonbinding. It also says the agency considers factors beyond the action levels when contemplating enforcement. A private sourcing page should not turn that guidance into a universal pass certificate. [FDA guidance, Introduction][FDA-LEAD]

For the buyer, the appropriate question is which rule governs this evaluation and why. If the answer is a category standard, open that standard. If the standard cites a regulator, follow the citation. If applicability is unresolved, preserve the gap. The work is not complete merely because both sides of a comparison contain numbers.

## Make a decision someone else can reconstruct

A useful decision record explains the material in scope, the evidence considered, the standard version and the conclusion. It also identifies limitations that affected the review. This is an editorial recommendation for sourcing practice, not a claim that a single form can establish compliance everywhere.

Imagine that a colleague takes over the purchase after the original reviewer leaves. Could that colleague explain why the material was accepted? Could they distinguish the approved scope from an assumption? Could they see whether new information requires another review? If the answer depends on an undocumented conversation, the record is not doing its job.

This is also why a missing result should not be represented as a favorable one. Absence of evidence and evidence supporting a decision are different states. A system that makes those states look identical gives the next reviewer a problem the first reviewer already knew about.

## Publication should clarify the boundary

Published standards allow a buyer to examine the rule without depending on a sales explanation. Access to relevant lot evidence lets the buyer examine the support for a particular claim. Where access is restricted, the restriction should be clear. Public availability of a standard does not imply public availability of a laboratory report.

VLE's stated approach is a standard per product category with scheduled retesting. Its existing model distinguishes supplier documentation from samples, authenticated evidence and qualification decisions. A file attached to a lot or profile is a reference to inspect. It is not a measured result stored by that attachment record, and it does not itself qualify the lot.

That distinction protects the meaning of the claim. A buyer should be able to see what has been established, what is merely attached and what remains unanswered. The category habit of saying tested obscures those differences. A better record makes them visible before money, material and responsibility move. Lot identity tells us what we are discussing. Evidence tells us what was examined. The standard tells us how to judge it. The decision must explain the connection.
