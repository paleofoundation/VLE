# "Tested" is not a result

A supplier page says the ingredient is tested. The buyer is expected to understand that as reassurance. But reassurance is not the information the buyer needs to make a decision. Which contaminant was measured? In what material? When was the sample taken? What did the laboratory find? What rule was used to decide whether that finding was acceptable?

If the page answers none of those questions, the word has done more work than the evidence. That is the category norm worth challenging: a claim that sounds specific while leaving the result and the standard out of view. The objection is not to testing. It is to asking a buyer to accept the existence of testing as a substitute for its meaning.

## Start with the question the test answered

A useful sourcing discussion starts with the ingredient and the intended use. The buyer needs to identify the material being offered, the relevant contaminant, and the category standard against which the evidence will be assessed. A report for another material or another purpose may be useful background. It cannot silently become the answer to this purchase.

The same discipline applies to a report described as comprehensive. The adjective does not establish its scope. Read the analyte list. Read the sample description. Check whether the document identifies the material under discussion. Ask what is missing before deciding what the report proves. A document can be authentic and still fail to answer the buyer's question.

This is a practical demand, not a request for a more impressive certificate. If the purchasing team cannot explain why a report applies, the approval rests on an assumption. Writing that assumption down often reveals the next question that needs an answer.

## A finding needs its method

Even a result described as not detected needs context. EFSA explains that a method's detection limit marks the concentration it can reliably detect; material below that level may go unnoticed. A non-detect therefore does not establish absence. The method's limit belongs beside the finding. [EFSA detection-limit explanation][EFSA-LOD]

The buyer should not have to reconstruct that context from a sales sentence. The laboratory's terminology, reported units and qualifications should travel with the result. If the supplier provides only a cropped page or a summary, request the missing material. An incomplete record should stay visibly incomplete until the question is resolved.

There is no benefit in making uncertainty disappear from the presentation when it remains in the evidence. It only moves the uncertainty to the buyer, who may now make a decision without knowing it was there.

## The standard must be available too

A result and a decision are different things. The result describes what was measured. The decision explains how that evidence was assessed under a defined rule. A claim of passing is difficult to examine if the rule is private, unnamed, or changed after the result arrived.

FDA's January 2025 guidance on lead in certain processed foods for babies and young children illustrates why scope matters. It distinguishes covered food categories and excludes others. It is nonbinding guidance, not a universal contaminant limit for any ingredient a buyer might purchase. [FDA guidance, Introduction and Action Levels][FDA-LEAD]

A buyer can use that distinction without borrowing a number from the document. Identify the applicable category, intended use, jurisdiction and version first. If those do not match, the source cannot be made applicable by putting its name beside a result. A published limit earns its usefulness through a defensible connection to the material being evaluated.

## Public claims need checkable support

Not all commercial paperwork belongs on a public page. Confidentiality is a real consideration. But a public claim still needs enough public explanation to establish its scope, and the decision maker needs access to the supporting record. A promise to provide something later is not the same as evidence already reviewed.

The boundary should be explicit. State what is available, what requires authorized access, and what has not been supplied. Do not let a document icon imply that the buyer has seen a result. Do not let an inaccessible link stand in for a review. If a claim cannot yet be supported, narrow the claim.

That is an editorial position about accountability. It does not assert that a regulator requires all laboratory reports to be public. It asks a simpler question: what can the person being asked to rely on this statement actually check?

## What we ask the record to carry

VLE's stated approach is a standard per product category with scheduled retesting. Within that approach, a supplier document, a sample, evidence and a qualification decision remain distinct. Attaching a file does not establish that the material has met the standard. The record must support the decision being made about the identified material.

That distinction also limits what we should say. Scheduled retesting describes an obligation under a category standard. It does not establish coverage of inventory outside the documented scope. A future retest date is not a new result. A missing date is not permission to invent one. The claim must remain smaller than, or equal to, the evidence supporting it.

A buyer should be able to leave the page with a precise statement: this is the material, this is the relevant evidence, this is the standard used, and these are the unanswered questions. If the record cannot support that statement, the next step is to obtain the missing evidence. The word tested should begin that conversation. It should never be allowed to end it.
